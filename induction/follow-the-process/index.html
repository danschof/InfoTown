    Sam's Heads Up

    One need, lots of parcels, two ways to read it

    One need from the client spawns a whole family: the requirements, the plan, the drawing, the issue, the TQ, the shop drawing, the inspection, the survey and the handover pack. Each is its own parcel with its own number, and each leaves its own trail. A real job runs thousands of these at once.

    By work stage is how the job feels: we join at Stage 4. By ISO 19650 process is what the information management is doing, and it shows something the stages hide: "make it together" and "deliver it" come round every time the drawing changes. Same gates, every time.

    The lines are the workflow. A parcel travelling along one is changing state: from work in progress to shared to published. Your towns might be separate systems or rooms in one system; the moves are the same, and the CDE records every one. Every town runs its own checkpoints before anything leaves, and approval (A) is its ticket out.

    Watch who moves. Parcels only travel when they change state. Everyone else comes to the Site Office to comment, authorise or accept, the Site Office records who did what, and then they go home and get on with it. When something changes, it changes where it sits and people are told. Nobody gets sent a copy.

    This is how this job does it. Yours might keep work in progress in the CDE, run its CDE across several systems, or use the client's own. That's all fine: see "Your project may do it differently" below.

    The codes are the UK ones from the National Annex to ISO 19650. A5 is authorised and accepted for Stage 5, and A6 for Stage 6; the reason for issue says why. Your client may use its own stage gates, and your project's information standard may use different codes, so check it.

    The rules Eight rules of the trail

    Drawing 042 has one revision history and many linked records. Everything else (issues, mark-ups, TQs, surveys, inspections, shop drawings, packs) is its own parcel, pointing back at the exact revision it relates to.

    1. The revision changes when the content changes. The status changes when the purpose changes. S4 to S5 on the same content is a change to the label in the CDE, not a re-issue.
    2. The status lives in the CDE. The sheet carries the revision and a PRELIMINARY or CONTRACTUAL stamp, matching the P or C. Where a client's title block insists on showing S codes, a status change becomes a content change: re-issue it with the description "Status change only, no technical change".
    3. A published code says which work stage it's accepted for (A5 is Stage 5, A6 is Stage 6), not why. So every published issue also carries a reason for issue from the project's information standard: For construction, For manufacture, Quality record, As-built survey, As-constructed record, Handover. (We say "as-built" in plain words; the reason for issue on the record drawing is As-constructed record.)
    4. Every town checks its own work before it leaves. Check, review and approve (the team's checkpoints) happen in work in progress, before anything moves to Shared, whoever made it. Authorise and accept (two more checkpoints: the lead firm authorises, then the client accepts) happen in the CDE, before anything moves to Published: the people come to the parcel, the parcel doesn't go to them, and the decision is recorded against the revision.
    5. Only the originator revises its own parcel, in its own room. A mark-up, issue or TQ never changes Drawing 042. It gives Alex a reason to.
    6. Once a drawing is contractual, rework goes back into work in progress as the next P revision: C01, then P04.01 and P04, then C02. Write this into the project's information standard.
    7. Feedback records carry a workflow state (Open, Responded, Closed), not a design status. If the project holds a mark-up as its own container, S2 is a sensible convention, never A.
    8. Every stop must answer "can you prove it?": who, when, which revision, and which record caused it.

    Supplier Town Shop drawings: who sees them

    A shop drawing is a separate parcel owned by whoever makes the thing, based on a published design revision. It's never the designer's next revision. Who reviews it depends on who carries the design responsibility, and the contract and responsibility matrix should say so before the first one arrives.

    WhoWhat they checkDo they always see it?
    Manufacturer (the originator)Buildability, tolerances, its own checks: the team's checkpointsYes, they produce it
    Designer of recordIt matches the design intent of the revision it's based on, with no new design assumptionsYes, where the item carries design intent: S3 for comment
    Lead firmInterfaces, logistics, programme, fit with everything elseYes: it authorises at S4 and releases it as A5, For manufacture
    ClientOnly where the contract says so: contractor-designed elements, specified assets, safety-critical itemsNot by default

    InfoTown's convention: here the lead firm authorises SD-118 at S4 and releases it as A5 because, by default, it's the accepting party for its own subcontract appointment. Where the client has listed shop drawings as deliverables it wants to review, they go on to S5 for the client to accept, like any other deliverable.

    The trail must show which design revision each shop drawing relied on. When 042 moved from C01 to C02, someone had to check SD-118 was still valid. Linking them in the CDE makes that check happen on purpose, not by luck. A firm that designs and makes (a precast firm doing contractor-designed work, say) still sits in Supplier Town, but its design submissions follow the design line's rules.

    The site Site packs

    A site pack is a list of links to exact published revisions, held in the CDE, not a folder of copies. It answers "what was Dave working from that day?".

    • Only published items go in: drawings at A5 For construction, shop drawings at A5 For manufacture, the inspection plan, RAMS, permits, and any instructions or TQ answers that apply.
    • Never in a pack: anything at an S status, red-lines, or superseded revisions.
    • When a linked item is superseded (042 C01 to C02), the pack is updated, the change is recorded against it and the site team is told. Printed copies say "uncontrolled when printed, check the CDE".
    • The pack has its own number and revision, so the trail can prove which version was on the tablet when the trench was dug.
    • At the end, the pack's inspections and as-built records feed the record drawing and the handover pack. Packs organise construction; they aren't the handover.
    Project choices Your project may do it differently

    The map shows one good way. The workflow is the idea that matters; the systems and the details are your project's choice, written into its information standard and BEP.

    ChoiceOn this pageAlso fine
    Where work in progress livesIn each team's own system, in its own town. The Site Office only knows what's been shared.Work-in-progress areas inside the CDE, one per team, so the CDE sees every version. The project decides.
    What the CDE isOne Site Office, with the site app as one of its cabinsSeveral systems: documents in a document system, models on a model server or in CAD, maps in GIS, a field app on site. It's still one CDE if it runs one workflow: the same states, labels and decisions, all recorded.
    Whose CDEA new Site Office, built by the lead firm because the client asked for oneThe client's own CDE. Then getting ready means getting your team access and testing your workflows in someone else's building.
    Where the client acceptsThe client's reviewer comes to the Site OfficeThe client accepts in its own system. Then the parcel does travel up the client line, as a formal transfer, and the decision comes back to be recorded.
    How people are toldOne copy; the status changes where it sits; a notification with a link goes to everyone on the distributionConnected systems pick up the new status through their own approved integrations, not side routes. If a system really needs its own copy (the client's document system, an offline tablet), it's a controlled transfer with the same number, revision and status, superseded when the original is. Never re-shared as a new parcel.

    ISO 19650 works with whatever stages your project uses: RIBA on buildings, the client's own gates on infrastructure. Your project's information standard should say which stages the A codes follow.

    An S code says what something may be used for. An A code only says it's been authorised and accepted, and the n is the work stage. The Annex ties n to the work stages in BS 8536-1 (buildings) and BS 8536-2 (infrastructure), since brought together in BS 8536:2022, unless your project's information standard sets out another approach. On this site, construction is stage 5 and handover is stage 6, so a construction issue is A5 and the as-built record is A6. Why each A code was issued isn't in the code at all: your project's information standard has to say.

    Revision codes

    CodeWhenWhat that means to you
    P01.01, P01.02…Work in progressStill being worked on. The second number is the version within that revision, so the team can keep track of its working versions before the next shared revision. Nobody outside the team should be using it.
    P01, P02…SharedA shared draft. The number goes up every time it's shared again, so check you've got the highest one.
    C01, C02…PublishedThe published version, authorised and accepted. Higher is newer. Build from the latest C, with an A status.
