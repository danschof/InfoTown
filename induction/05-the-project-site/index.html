The right way, explained

A project is a shared place where several organisations work together for a while: the project site. Someone has to build it before day one: the shared system (the Site Office), the links to every firm (the lines) and a guide to where things go (the signposts). Sam did all three two weeks early, so Rex was useful by lunchtime.

The scene

A shared place, just for this job

In Lesson 3 you met the firms on a job: the client, the firm leading the work, and the firms doing it. Each has its own town, and nobody moves into anyone else's. Instead, they set up one shared place for this job: the project site. Nobody owns it, everybody works through it, and it's taken down when the job ends. Before anyone can use it, it has to be built, connected and signposted. That's mobilisation.

Now mobilise it: build and connect the site

In the middle sits the Site Office: the project's CDE, an extension of your own Records Office. Lines run out to every town: the design line to the designers, the client line to the client, the home line back to your own Records Office, the Underground to the site apps and footpaths to the cabins. The workflows running along all these lines are how one system passes work to another. Signposts tell everyone where each kind of work goes, and the gate decides who gets in.

Sam's Heads Up

Your Site Office might be three buildings in a trench coat

Here the Site Office is one tidy building. On your job, the CDE might be one system, or three systems pretending to be one, with a shared drive lurking round the back.

Find out which before day one: what goes where, which one is the real record, and who decided. If nobody can tell you, that's your first job.

The reveal

This is Mobilisation!

Setting up, connecting and testing the project's systems, access and routes before the real work begins.

In ISO 19650, mobilisation is when the delivery team gets its CDE, its connections, its people's access and its ways of working in place, and tests them, so information can flow from day one. Controlling who can get in, and what they can see, is part of a security-minded approach.
