Think like Stevie

A question to the designer is information, and so is the answer. Ask where the rest of the job can see it, quote the drawing and its revision, and don't build until the answer is on the record or the drawing.

The exchange map

Which line, which parcels, and when

Every project needs a map of its proper routes: who sends what, to whom, and when. Drawings come in on the design line, go out to site on the site line, and up to the client for acceptance. If it's on the map, everyone knows where to look. The back alley never is. Want to see a real job ride it? Follow the Process.

This is a Technical Query!

A question to the designer, and the answer, both on the record, and the drawing changed to match.

Sam's Heads Up Phone chats aren't the enemy

InfoTown good practice: Questions and answers that change the work go through the CDE.

On your job: Quick chats on a phone will happen, and they're useful. The rule is that anything that changes what gets built ends up on the CDE, as a query and an answer, the same day.

For the audit What the auditor will ask
  • Show me how a change agreed on site got onto the drawing.
  • Where are your questions to the designer, and their answers, kept?
For the audit What good evidence looks like
  • A query log on the CDE, each with its drawing, revision, answer and dates.
  • Revised drawings that point back to the query that changed them.

From the standard
ISO 19650 Exchanging information

ISO 19650 expects information to be exchanged through the CDE, so there's a record of what was shared, by whom and when. Technical queries are a common way to do that on site; the name and the form are your project's, not the standard's.

Based on the ISO 19650 series: the ideas come from the standard; the plain-English wording, examples and advice are InfoTown's. ISO 19650 is © ISO and BSI. You'll hear the same language across the industry. Read more for free in the Information Management Initiative (IMI) Framework guidance from BSI and nima.
