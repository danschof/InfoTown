The right way, explained

The right way, explained

When a building is finished, the town files its records in the archive and takes the keys back. Leave the door open and the keys out, and either anyone wanders in or someone clears it out by mistake.

If you're the project manager

If you're the project manager

Put close-out in the programme, with an owner, before the team starts leaving. The last week of a job is too late.

In the town See it in the town: the job's over. What do you keep, and who still has a key?

Sam's view: the Records Office's back room. Keep-until dates on every shelf, and the keys hung back on the hook.

  1. 1 What has to be kept, kept for as long as it might be needed, with a keep-until date on every shelf. Working files are closed, not kept "just in case".
  2. 2 Every key handed back: nobody who has left the job can still get in.
  3. 3 The lessons, each with an owner, carried into the next bid rather than left in a folder.

For the audit What the auditor will ask
  • Show me how this project was closed: what was archived, where, until when, and who signed it off.
  • Who can still access it?
For the audit What good evidence looks like
  • A signed close-out checklist.
  • An archive with an index and keep-until dates.
  • Access removed.
  • A lessons log with owners, and proof it fed the next bid.

Where Rex comes in: Lesson 11: Using Information

From the standard
ISO 19650 Close-out

At the end of the job, ISO 19650 expects the project's information to be archived properly and lessons learned to be captured for future projects. How long to keep it is set by your contract, the client's needs and your own policy.

Close-outIn plain words
ArchiveThe record that matters, indexed and findable
Keep untilLong enough to cover defects and liability periods
Close and removeWorking areas closed, access removed
LessonsCaptured with owners, and used on the next job
